\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
An agent is represented by a local probability law together with mechanisms for receiving records, making predictions, and changing that law. Gauge transformations change its presentation. Interactions communicate selected information between agents and may depend on their current belief and model laws. We formulate these objects on associated statistical bundles, give explicit law-dependent links, and distinguish a normalized comparison likelihood from a direct divergence between peer laws. A coupled two-sector variational dynamics follows from a declared potential and the Fisher metric. Exact conditional-information identities describe what communication preserves and what a receiver supplies through its own model. A parent is characterized by the information required to inherit future evolution and boundary responses. Exact quotient criteria and counterexamples identify this requirement. Under specified conditional reconstruction channels, log-density spectra give an exact general-law completion of the original smooth dynamics, including a declared class of feedback. A finite example with a prescribed smooth erasure gate gives persistent groups of distinct children and exact inherited public evolution. It discards private degrees of freedom while retaining every public child state. A separate nonsmooth extension gives two successive stages of finite-time fusion in a specified lognormal family. Endogenous sufficient-interface selection, further aggregation of distinct public states, recursive formation under the original smooth dynamics, and the derivation of base geometry remain open.

\section*{Reading the construction}
The mathematical objects are introduced in the order in which they are needed: agent laws and records, interactions, dynamics, predictive compression, and inherited organization. The main results include their decisive proofs. The appendices give the internal history construction, the relation between directional links and a base connection, and a notation table. Secondary calculations are collected in a separate supplement.

Definitions specify the model's objects; constitutive choices specify how those objects interact. A theorem states a consequence of explicit hypotheses. An open problem identifies a further construction that is not supplied by those hypotheses. General probability measures are used whenever measurability suffices. Finite alphabets, smooth statistical families, and vector fibers enter only where an example or a differential calculation requires them. The general definitions assume no Gaussian closure; the fusion example states its lognormal restriction explicitly.
