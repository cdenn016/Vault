## Contribution and scope

Fields and Levin examine meaning as a relation between a living system's discriminations and the actions those discriminations enable. They use reference frames to explain how a system compares inputs with stable standards, drawing examples from bacterial chemotaxis, object recognition, attention, and memory. A simple organism can assign different practical consequences to environmental states without representing persistent objects or using human language. More complex systems must also allocate attention among competing stimuli and retain information that supports later action. The review argues that these functions provide a vocabulary spanning biological scales, with particular molecular or neural implementations at each scale. Its extension of cognitive language across life is a theoretical synthesis of diverse evidence. It does not supply a single experimentally established mechanism implementing all meanings, nor require that a signal's significance be an intrinsic property recoverable independently of the receiving system.

## Relevance to this research

This is directly relevant to [[Cue-result generative memory]] and [[Collective active inference]]: the receiver's retained organization determines which distinctions in a cue change its behavior. It also clarifies why coordination does not require identical internal representations. For [[Gauge-Theoretic Multi-Agent VFE Model]], semantic interpretation should be tied to specified conditional responses and records, with any claim about a shared meaning distinguished from an equality of normalized probability laws.
