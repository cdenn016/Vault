## Contribution and scope

Fields and Levin recast the origin-of-life problem as partly a problem of recognizing and interacting with unfamiliar agents. They combine their interpretation of the Conway–Kochen theorem with the free-energy principle to argue that persistent physical systems can be described as minimally agentive and inferential. On this view, the distinction between life, cognition, and nonliving organization need not have a single sharp boundary. The paper applies that perspective to synthetic life, the Fermi paradox, and the Drake equation, emphasizing the probability that an observer recognizes a pattern as communication. Its proposed change of viewpoint prioritizes successful interaction over resemblance to terrestrial biochemistry. This is a conceptual argument with no new experimental dataset. The broad claim that life and cognition are ubiquitous is the authors' interpretation of a combination of physical and inferential assumptions; it is not a direct statement of the Conway–Kochen theorem itself.

## Relevance to this research

The operational emphasis is useful for [[Collective active inference]] and for defining what counts as an agent in [[Gauge-Theoretic Multi-Agent VFE Model]]. Recognition depends on an observer's available distinctions and response repertoire. A normalized interacting-agent model can express such dependence without assuming that every persistent system is alive or conscious. The quantum theorem, the Bayesian modeling interpretation, and the proposed biological vocabulary should remain separately identified.
