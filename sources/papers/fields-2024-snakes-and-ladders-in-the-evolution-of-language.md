## Contribution and scope

Fields and Levin use the persistence of tool-making traditions to distinguish individual innovation from a durable change in a group. A person can invent and successfully use a tool without other people learning its affordances, manufacturing procedure, or value. A lasting tradition therefore depends on coordination among bodies, capabilities, memories, and social learning processes. The authors connect this requirement with federated inference and argue that shared practices and language must be considered at group scale. They also emphasize that embodied capacities constrain which predictions can become useful actions: a model of an action does not provide the morphology needed to perform it. This short commentary applies an active-inference perspective to an existing paleoanthropological argument. It does not supply new archaeological evidence, a dated reconstruction of language origins, or a mathematical proof that every successful innovation necessarily produces a shared generative model.

## Relevance to this research

The paper is useful context for [[Collective active inference]] because it makes uptake and persistence separate from sending a message or discovering a behavior. In [[Gauge-Theoretic Multi-Agent VFE Model]], durable coordination would need explicit recipient learning and a mechanism retaining the resulting capacities. Its language of shared inference should not be treated as an exact identity between peer-belief KL penalties and the evidence bound of a single normalized joint model.
