# The Architecture of Complexity

> [!info] Citation
> Herbert A. Simon (1962). "The Architecture of Complexity." *Proceedings of the American
> Philosophical Society* **106**(6), 467–482.

## TL;DR

The founding argument that observed complex systems are hierarchies because hierarchies are what
evolution can build. The watchmakers parable — Hora assembles through stable subassemblies and
finishes; Tempus must get everything right at once and almost never does — shows that assembly
through stable intermediate forms is overwhelmingly faster than all-at-once assembly, so complex
systems that exist are overwhelmingly those that grew stagewise. The companion concept is
**near-decomposability**: interactions within subsystems are strong and fast, interactions between
subsystems weak and slow, so each level admits an approximate theory of its own.

## What it establishes

Two claims the paper argues rather than proves formally: hierarchy is selected by assembly
kinetics (stable subassemblies make the search cumulative), and near-decomposable systems admit
level-wise description with inter-level coupling entering only through aggregate variables. Both
are stated for physical, biological, social, and symbolic systems alike.

## Relevance to this research

This is the canonical staged-assembly reference behind the intuition that agent hierarchies grow
through pairs and triples rather than forming wholesale — the kinetic side of what the finite
laboratory's C3 refutation says about descriptions (see
[[Staged hierarchy formation and RG composability]]). Near-decomposability is also the informal
ancestor of the block-formation criteria in [[Meta-agents and hierarchical emergence]]: strong
within-block coherence against weak boundary coupling is exactly what the culture-closure
diagnostic and the thin-boundary contraction measurements quantify.
