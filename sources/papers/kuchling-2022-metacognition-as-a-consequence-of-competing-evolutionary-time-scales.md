## Contribution and scope

Kuchling, Fields, and Levin define a metaprocessor as a regulator equipped with local memory and use that definition to relate metacognition to adaptation on multiple timescales. A metaprocessor can retain a history of performance and adjust an object-level process, rather than respond only to the current input. The authors argue that such organization can be energetically preferable when selective pressures vary at different rates. They connect the argument with active-inference networks, predator–prey models, coupled genetic algorithms, and generative adversarial networks, emphasizing that interacting adaptive systems can generate new characteristic timescales. Their usage of metacognition is architectural and broader than conscious reflection on one's thinking. The claimed prevalence of metacognitive organization follows under assumptions about resources, memory, and temporally structured selection; it is not an observation that all evolved systems contain a separately identifiable executive module or share a single learning algorithm.

## Relevance to this research

This is useful prior work for [[Meta-agents and hierarchical emergence]] because it specifies a retained state and a regulatory relation for a higher-level process. It motivates distinguishing fast inference, slower adaptation, and changes to the adaptation rule. In [[Gauge-Theoretic Multi-Agent VFE Model]], these roles still need explicit transition laws; a timescale separation alone does not establish exact probabilistic closure or an endogenous parent agent.
