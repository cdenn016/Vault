## TL;DR

A Markov process whose transition law commutes with the action of a finite group has an informationally closed macroscopic level given by the group orbits, and each subgroup supplies a further closed level, so the subgroup lattice orders a hierarchy of such levels. When the observation channel and the prior share the symmetry, the orbit variables of state and measurement form a hidden Markov model of their own, so a Bayesian observer can run exact filtering on a low-dimensional belief about the macroscopic variable without tracking the microstate. The preprint illustrates the first half with Hopfield networks (Mattis magnetization and the dihedral symmetries of stored patterns) and the second with Ehrenfest diffusion (particle count under the permutation group).

## Problem & setting

The paper starts from the observation that systems of interest show nested levels with their own regularities and that scientific knowledge is organized along the same levels. Renormalization group theory explains separation of scales near criticality, and information-theoretic approaches to multiscale dynamics (Granger-causal autonomy and emergence, causal emergence, dynamical independence) give criteria for when a coarse-grained level is self-contained; the author's position is that these criteria do not identify a mechanism that produces such levels and say little about why emergence helps learning and inference. The proposal is that dynamical equivariance under a symmetry group is such a mechanism, for the process and for an observer's beliefs alike. The standing setting is a stochastic process $X_t \in \mathcal{X}$ observed at discrete times $t \in \mathbb{Z}$; the presentation restricts to finite groups and discrete random variables, stated on p. 2 as a simplification. Theorem 1 is stated for general processes with dependence on the whole past, and the corollaries specialize to Markov processes. The observer of Section IV has access only to measurements $Y_t$ generated by a hidden Markov model and updates beliefs by exact Bayesian filtering. The work builds on information closure (Bertschinger et al. 2006; Chang et al. 2020), on dynamical independence (Barnett and Seth 2023) and on the author's earlier paper with coauthors on computational closure, cited as reference [6] and held in the vault as [[rosas-2024-software-natural-world]].

## Method

A coarse-graining $\phi: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Z}$ defines $Z_t = \phi(X_t)$. With $\vec{X}_t = (\dots, X_{t-1}, X_t)$ and $\vec{Z}^L_{t+1} = (Z_{t+1}, \dots, Z_{t+L})$, the coarse-graining is called emergent when it satisfies information closure (Eq. 1): for all $L \in \mathbb{N}$ and $t \in \mathbb{Z}$,

$$
I(\vec{X}_t; \vec{Z}^L_{t+1}) - I(\vec{Z}_t; \vec{Z}^L_{t+1}) = 0.
$$

The paper states that this is equivalent to Eq. (2), $\mathbb{E}\{\log [p(\vec{Z}^L_{t+1} \mid \vec{X}_t) / p(\vec{Z}^L_{t+1} \mid \vec{Z}_t)]\} = 0$, a Kullback-Leibler divergence whose vanishing gives $p(\vec{Z}^L_{t+1} \mid \vec{X}_t) = p(\vec{Z}^L_{t+1} \mid \vec{Z}_t)$ almost surely. A group $G$ acts on $\mathcal{X}$ through maps satisfying $(g_1 g_2) \cdot x = g_1 \cdot (g_2 \cdot x)$, and its orbits $O_x := \{x' \in \mathcal{X} : g \cdot x = x', g \in G\}$ (Eq. 3) partition the state space, giving the orbit coarse-graining $\phi_G(x) := O_x$. The Reynolds operator is the group average $\mathcal{R}\{\mu\} = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} g \cdot \mu$ (Eq. 4). Applied to the conditional law of future blocks $K^L_{t+1}(\vec{x}_t) := p(\vec{x}^L_{t+1} \mid \vec{x}_t)$ with a componentwise action of $G^L$, it gives Eq. (5),

$$
\mathcal{R}\{K^L_{t+1}\}(\vec{x}_t) = \frac{1}{|G|^L} \sum_{\vec{g}^L_{t+1} \in G^L} p(\vec{g}^L_{t+1} \cdot \vec{x}^L_{t+1} \mid \vec{x}_t).
$$

A Markov process is called dynamically equivariant when there is a group action with $p(g \cdot x_{t+1} \mid g \cdot x_t) = p(x_{t+1} \mid x_t)$ for all states and all $g \in G$ (Eq. 8).

For the observer, a hidden Markov model has joint law $p(\vec{x}^{t+1}_0, \vec{y}^t_0) = p(x_0) \prod_{\tau=0}^{t} p(x_{\tau+1} \mid x_\tau) p(y_\tau \mid x_\tau)$ (Eq. 20). An equivariant hidden Markov model carries two actions of the same group, one on $\mathcal{X}$ and one on $\mathcal{Y}$, with (Eqs. 21a-21c)

$$
p(g \cdot x_{t+1} \mid g \cdot x_t) = p(x_{t+1} \mid x_t), \qquad p(g \cdot y_t \mid g \cdot x_t) = p(y_t \mid x_t), \qquad p(g \cdot x_0) = p(x_0).
$$

The orbit variables are $Z_t = \phi_G(X_t)$ and $V_t = \psi_G(Y_t)$ (Eq. 22). Bayesian beliefs are the process $B_t := \mathbb{E}\{\mathbb{1}_{X_t} \mid \vec{Y}_t\}$ with realizations $b_t(x_t) = p(x_t \mid \vec{y}_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|}$, updated by the filtering recursion of Eq. (23),

$$
b_t = \mathrm{Norm}(\mathrm{diag}(L_{y_t}) T_t b_{t-1}) =: U(y_t, b_{t-1}),
$$

where $T_t$ is the transition matrix with entries $p(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$, $L_{y_t}$ is the likelihood vector with entries $p(y_t \mid x_t)$ and $\mathrm{Norm}(v) = v / \sum_j v_j$. For any coarse-graining $W_t = f(X_t)$ the coarse belief is $B^f_t = P_f B_t$ with $[P_f]_{w,x} = \mathbb{1}\{w = f(x)\}$, but the paper notes that $B^f_t$ is in general not Markovian and so has no recursion of the form of Eq. (23). The map from micro-level beliefs to abstract beliefs is defined in Eqs. (26)-(28), writing $b_t[\vec{y}_t] = p(x_t \mid \vec{y}_t)$:

$$
\Psi_G\{b_t[\vec{y}_t]\} := \mathbb{E}\{\mathbb{1}_{Z_t} \mid V_t = \psi_G(\vec{y}_t)\} = P_{\phi_G}\Big( \sum_{\vec{y}'_t : \psi_G(\vec{y}'_t) = \psi_G(\vec{y}_t)} c_{\vec{y}'_t} b_t[\vec{y}'_t] \Big), \qquad c_{\vec{y}'_t} = p(\vec{y}'_t \mid \psi_G(\vec{y}_t)) = \frac{p(\vec{y}'_t)}{\sum_{\vec{y}'_t : \psi_G(\vec{y}'_t) = \psi_G(\vec{y}_t)} p(\vec{y}'_t)}.
$$

The map first mixes the micro-level beliefs over all measurement sequences in the same symmetry class and then marginalizes with $P_{\phi_G}$ (the conditioning variable in Eq. 26 is printed as $V_t$ without the trajectory arrow).

The two case studies are specified as follows. The Hopfield network has neurons $X^i_t \in \{-1, 1\}$ updated in parallel with $\mathbb{P}\{X^i_{t+1} = 1 \mid X^1_t, \dots, X^n_t\} = F(\beta \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_{i,j} X^j_t)$, $F(x) = (1 + \tanh x)/2$ (Eq. 14), and Hebbian weights $w_{i,j} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_\mu q^i_\mu q^j_\mu$ for $i \neq j$ and $0$ otherwise (Eq. 15) for $m$ patterns $q_\mu \in \{-1, 1\}^n$. The Ehrenfest model has $n$ particles with $X^k_t \in \{0, 1\}$ marking the chamber of particle $k$, transition law $p(x_{t+1} \mid x_t) = 1 - q$ if $x_{t+1} = x_t$, $q/n$ if the Hamming distance $\|x_{t+1} - x_t\|$ equals $1$, and $0$ otherwise (Eq. 33), and measurements through a binary symmetric channel with crossover probability $r$, $p(y_t \mid x_t) = \prod_{k=1}^{n} r^{\mathbb{1}\{x^k_t \neq y^k_t\}} (1 - r)^{\mathbb{1}\{x^k_t = y^k_t\}}$ (Eq. 34).

## Key results

Theorem 1 (p. 2) states that the orbit coarse-graining $Z_t = \phi_G(X_t)$ is informationally closed if and only if $\mathcal{R}\{K^L_{t+1}\}(\vec{x}_t)$ is invariant under the action of $G$ on the past trajectory. The proof writes $p(\vec{z}^L_{t+1} \mid \vec{x}_t) = |\phi^{-1}_{G^L}(\vec{z}^L_{t+1})| \mathcal{R}\{K^L_{t+1}\}(\vec{x}_t)$ (Eqs. 6-7) using the orbit-stabilizer theorem, and identifies invariance of the left side under the action on the past with Eq. (2). Corollary 1 (p. 2): if a Markov process is dynamically equivariant, then $\phi_G(X_t)$ is informationally closed; the proof (Eqs. 9-12) absorbs the group element acting on $x_t$ into the summation over $G^L$. Endnote [22] qualifies the corollary: equivariance is sufficient for emergence and not necessary, and the endnote names Markov lumpability as a necessary condition, describing it as a local symmetry in the formalism of fibration symmetries. Endnote [20] observes that every coarse-graining of a discrete state space arises from some group: for a partition with cells $C_i$, take $G_i$ the group of permutations of $C_i$ and $G_\phi = \prod_{i} G_i$; the partition is the orbit partition of $G_\phi$. The paper adds that for deterministic systems with random initial conditions the corollary generalizes the classical factoring-out of symmetries of equivariant maps (Field 1980; Golubitsky, Stewart and Schaeffer).

Corollary 2 (p. 3): if $X_t$ is a Markov process equivariant with respect to $G$ and $H$ is a subgroup, then with $Z_t = \phi_G(X_t)$ and $Z'_t = \phi_H(X_t)$ the process $Z_t$ is informationally closed with respect to both $X_t$ and $Z'_t$. The second part follows from the data processing inequality (Eq. 13), $0 \le I(\vec{Z}'_t; \vec{Z}^L_{t+1}) - I(\vec{Z}_t; \vec{Z}^L_{t+1}) \le I(\vec{X}_t; \vec{Z}^L_{t+1}) - I(\vec{Z}_t; \vec{Z}^L_{t+1}) = 0$. Since orbits of $G$ are unions of orbits of $H$ whenever $H \subseteq G$, the paper describes an order-preserving map, which it calls a homomorphism, from the subgroup lattice to the informationally closed coarse-grainings, with $G$ giving the coarsest level and $\{e\}$ the identity; the map can collapse distinct subgroups. Figure 1 illustrates this with a six-state Markov chain on states $V_1, V_2, V_3, M_1, M_2, M_3$ that has the symmetries of a triangle, the dihedral group $D_3$. As read from Fig. 1A, each $V$ state stays with probability $0.5$ and moves to each neighboring $M$ state with probability $0.25$, and each $M$ state moves to each neighboring $V$ state with probability $0.5$. Fig. 1B maps the six subgroups $D_3$, $\langle r \rangle$, $\langle s \rangle$, $\langle sr \rangle$, $\langle sr^2 \rangle$, $\{e\}$ to five coarse-grainings, with $\phi_{D_3} = \phi_{\langle r \rangle}$ and $\phi_{\{e\}} = \mathrm{Id}$.

In the Hopfield case study (pp. 3-5) the input to neuron $i$ is rewritten as $\sum_{j=1}^{n} w_{i,j} X^j_t = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{\mu=1}^{m} q^i_\mu \sum_{j=1}^{m} q^j_\mu X^j_t$ (Eq. 16, as printed), which makes the Mattis magnetization $Z^\mu_t = \frac{1}{n}(q_\mu \cdot X_t) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} q^j_\mu X^j_t$ (Eq. 17) the order parameter. The group $G$ is taken to be all permutations $\sigma$ of neurons that leave every magnetization unchanged, $(q_\mu \cdot (\sigma \cdot x)) = (q_\mu \cdot x)$ for all $\mu$ and all $x \in \{-1, 1\}^n$ (Eq. 18). The paper states that the magnetization is then the orbit variable, $Z_t = \phi_G(X_t)$, and that $p(\sigma \cdot x_{t+1} \mid \sigma \cdot x_t) = \prod_{j=1}^{n} p(x^{\sigma(j)}_{t+1} \mid g \cdot x_t) = \prod_{j=1}^{n} p(x^j_{t+1} \mid x_t)$ (Eq. 19, as printed), the second equality because the transition law depends on $x_t$ only through the magnetization. By Corollary 1 the magnetization is informationally closed, which the paper reads as saying that the choice of the retrieved pattern is made at the macroscopic level and depends on $Z_0$ and not on the further detail in $X_0$. For patterns consisting of all reflections and $90^\circ$ rotations of $k_0$ asymmetric letters on a square grid ($m = 8 k_0$), the weights and the dynamics acquire the symmetries of the dihedral group $D_4$, generated by the rotation $r$ and the horizontal reflection $s$, and $Z'_t = \phi_{D_4}(Z_t)$ records the evidence for each letter regardless of rotation or reflection. The eight proper nontrivial subgroups, three of order 4 (the cyclic group $\langle r \rangle$ and two copies of the Klein group, $\langle s, sr^2 \rangle$ and $\langle sr, sr^3 \rangle$) and five of order 2 (generated by $r^2$, $s$, $sr$, $sr^2$, $sr^3$, endnote [36]), give further closed levels; factoring by $\langle r \rangle$ keeps letter identity and whether the letter is reflected. In the numerical experiment, networks of $20 \times 20$ neurons were trained on the rotations and reflections of the letters G, J and R ($m = 3 \times 8 = 24$ patterns), and the state of $\phi_H(Z_t)$ at various times was used to predict itself after 250 timesteps. Fig. 2E plots prediction error on a logarithmic axis against timestep (0 to 200) for $D_4$, $\langle s, sr^2 \rangle$ and $\langle r \rangle$; the symmetry-derived coarse-grainings (solid) fall below randomly chosen coarse-grainings of the same cardinality (dotted). No numerical values are tabulated. Appendix B (p. 12) explains that the experiment used the pseudo-inverse learning rule (Kanter and Sompolinsky 1987) in place of Eq. (15), $W = \frac{1}{m} Q (\frac{1}{m} Q^\top Q)^{-1} Q^\top$ (Eq. B1) with $Q = [q_1, \dots, q_m]$ an $n \times m$ matrix, $m < n$, so that each pattern is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $1$ and $W x_t = \sum_{\mu=1}^{m} (h_\mu \cdot x_t) q_\mu$ (Eq. B2) with $h_\mu$ the rows of $(Q^\top Q)^{-1} Q^\top$. The generalized Mattis magnetization is $Z^\mu_t := (h_\mu \cdot X_t)/n$ (Eq. B3, as printed; the paper defines $(a \cdot b) = \sum_i a_i b_i / n$ as a normalized dot product, so the printed form divides by $n$ twice), recovered from the orbits of permutations $\rho$ with $(h_\mu (\rho x)) = (h_\mu x)$ for all $\mu$ and $x$ (Eq. B4); equivariance of the dynamics under this group is asserted.

Theorem 2 (p. 5, proof in Appendix A): if $(X_t, Y_t)$ is a hidden Markov model satisfying Eq. (21), then $(Z_t, V_t)$ is also a hidden Markov model. The proof shows $p(z_{t+1} \mid g \cdot x_t) = p(z_{t+1} \mid x_t)$ (Eqs. A1-A3) and $p(v_t \mid g \cdot x_t) = p(v_t \mid x_t)$ (Eq. A4), hence $p(z_{t+1} \mid x_t) = p(z_{t+1} \mid z_t)$ and $p(v_t \mid x_t) = p(v_t \mid z_t)$, and peels factors off the joint law to reach $p(\vec{z}^{t+1}_0, \vec{v}^t_0) = p(z_0) \prod_{\tau=0}^{t} p(z_{\tau+1} \mid z_\tau) p(v_\tau \mid z_\tau)$ (Eq. A5). Corollary 3 (p. 6): under the same conditions the abstract belief $b^*_t := p(z_t \mid \vec{v}_t)$ obeys $b^*_t = U(v_t, b^*_{t-1})$ and satisfies $b^*_t = P_{\phi_G} \mathbb{E}\{B_t \mid \vec{V}_t = \vec{v}_t\}$ (Eq. 24), since $b^*_t = \sum_{\vec{y}_t \in \psi^{-1}(\vec{v}_t)} p(\vec{y}_t \mid \vec{v}_t) p(z_t \mid \vec{y}_t)$ (Eq. 25). The paper stresses that the mixing formula of Eq. (27) holds for the beliefs of any coarse-graining of the latent process and that what distinguishes the symmetry-derived ones is the recursion. Corollary 4 (p. 7): if in addition

$$
I(Z_t; \vec{Y}_t) - I(Z_t; \vec{V}_t) = 0 \qquad \text{(Eq. 29)},
$$

then $b^*_t = P_{\phi_G} b_t$, so the abstract belief is the marginal of the micro-level belief (Eqs. 30-31). The paper gives $p(z_t \mid \vec{y}_t) = p(z_t \mid g_0 \cdot y_0, \dots, g_t \cdot y_t)$ for all $g_k \in G$ (Eq. 32) as an equivalent condition, and endnote [38] calls the result a generalization of the statement in reference [6] that information closure implies computational closure. Under Eq. (29) a square diagram commutes: the top arrow $b^*_t \to b^*_{t+1}$ is the abstract belief update $U(v_t, \cdot)$, the bottom arrow $b_t \to b_{t+1}$ is the micro-level belief update (labeled $U(z_t, \cdot)$ as printed), and both vertical arrows are $P_{\phi_G}$. When Eq. (29) fails, $I(Z_t; \vec{Y}_t) > I(Z_t; \vec{V}_t)$ and $P_{\phi_G} b_t$ is more informative about $Z_t$ than $b^*_t$, but $(Z_t, Y_t)$ need not be a hidden Markov model, so $p(z_t \mid \vec{y}_t)$ may have no efficient update that avoids $b_t$; the abstract belief is then the less informative and more tractable choice. Endnote [39] adds that, by the tower property and Eq. (24), $b^*_t$ and $P_{\phi_G} b_t$ agree in mean while the variance of $b^*_t$ is in general larger.

In the Ehrenfest case study (pp. 7-9) the micro-level belief has dimension $|\mathcal{X}| = 2^n$ and one pass of Eq. (23) costs $\mathcal{O}(2^{2n})$ operations; the paper states that a single update on a machine running at $10^{18}$ FLOPS would take around fourteen days for $n = 40$ and around forty thousand years for $n = 50$. The dynamics are equivariant under the permutation group $S_n$ acting by $\sigma \cdot x_t = (x^{\sigma(1)}_t, \dots, x^{\sigma(n)}_t)$ (Eq. 35), the orbit variable is the particle count $Z_t = \phi_{S_n}(X_t) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} X^k_t$, which follows a birth-death process, and the channel satisfies $p(\sigma \cdot y_t \mid \sigma \cdot x_t) = p(y_t \mid x_t)$. With a permutation-invariant initial condition, Eq. (21) holds, so by Theorem 2 the pair $Z_t$, $V_t = \sum_{k=1}^{n} Y^k_t$ is a hidden Markov model and by Corollary 3 the abstract belief $b^*_t = p(z_t \mid \vec{v}_t)$, of dimension $n + 1$, can be filtered directly; the stated cost for $n = 40$ or $n = 50$ on the same machine is no more than a few femtoseconds. The paper states that Eq. (29) is not satisfied in this model even with a permutation-invariant prior, so here the abstract belief is not the marginal of the micro-level belief, and reports that numerical evaluation confirmed the mapping of Eq. (27). Fig. 4D shows both beliefs for $n = 8$. The subgroup lattice of $S_n$ gives intermediate closed levels in which permutations are allowed only within blocks, so that disjoint groups of $M$ molecules become units $z^k_t \in \{0, \dots, M\}$; these levels can be tracked with measurements summed over the same blocks, and the paper likens the construction to the merging of degrees of freedom in renormalization.

The discussion (p. 9) positions the work against three bodies of method. Renormalization group theory explains emergence near criticality through scale invariance, and effective field theory through the filtering of irrelevant degrees of freedom; there symmetry determines the compatible operators and constrains the emergent laws, whereas here the symmetries are symmetries of the dynamics and determine the coarse-graining maps directly. Standard hierarchical Bayesian modeling places its hierarchy in parameter space and in general cannot update a coarse description without the finer layers. Variational inference gains efficiency by restricting the model class at the cost of approximation error, whereas abstract beliefs give exact inference by restricting the target of prediction; the paper suggests that future work combine the two, citing ongoing efforts that combine variational methods and renormalization (reference [50], on scale-free active inference). It also relates the correspondence between process and belief to the good regulator theorem of Conant and Ashby and to recent categorical treatments, points to abstraction in partially observable reinforcement learning and to equivariant architectures as applications. It suggests symmetry as a further route to identifying emergent levels and names more general forms of weak symmetry, citing work on symmetry fibrations, as a route to efficient graph-coloring algorithms and wider applicability. Whether symmetry is relevant to other notions of emergence (emergence focused on enhanced controllability or on whole-part relationships) is left open.

## Limits

The paper restricts the presentation to finite groups and discrete random variables and works in discrete time throughout; Corollaries 1 and 2 concern Markov processes and Section IV concerns hidden Markov models with exact Bayesian updating. No statement is made for continuous groups, continuous state spaces or continuous time. Equivariance is a sufficient condition only (endnote [22]), and the endnote's claims about lumpability and fibration symmetries come without proof or citation at that point. Other statements made without proof are: the equivalence of Eqs. (1) and (2); endnote [20]; the description of the subgroup-to-level map as a homomorphism, where only monotonicity is argued; the identification of the Mattis magnetization with the orbit variable, the second equality of Eq. (19), the $D_4$ equivariance and the equivariance claimed in Appendix B; the equivalence of Eq. (32) with Eq. (29) and the commuting diagram; the failure of Eq. (29) in the Ehrenfest model; and the numerical confirmation of Eq. (27), for which no data are shown. The Hopfield experiment is described without its predictor, error measure, value of $\beta$ or number of runs; Appendix B gives only the learning rule. When Eq. (29) fails, as the paper says it does in its own second case study, the abstract belief is strictly less informative than the marginalized micro-level belief, so the commuting diagram is not a general property of equivariant hidden Markov models. Statements, numbering and examples in the published version may differ from the preprint and were not checked; per the abstract of that unread version it describes symmetry as a sufficient mechanism and treats two further systems.

> [!note] Editorial: reader's observations, checked by hand derivation only
> The group of Eq. (18) consists of the permutations of neurons that fix every pattern, and its orbit variable is the vector of sums of $x$ over classes of neurons with identical pattern entries. That variable can be strictly finer than the magnetization: for $n = 2$, $m = 1$, $q = (1, -1)$ the group is trivial while $(1, 1)$ and $(-1, -1)$ share magnetization zero. The identity $Z_t = \phi_G(X_t)$ therefore holds only up to this refinement, and closure of the magnetization itself rests on the direct argument that the kernel depends on $x_t$ through $z_t$. With the zero diagonal of Eq. (15) the input to neuron $i$ is $\sum_\mu q^i_\mu Z^\mu_t - (m/n) X^i_t$, so Eq. (16) and that direct argument are exact when the diagonal weights are kept and hold up to a self-term of order $m/n$ when they are set to zero. The kernel is equivariant under the pattern-fixing permutations in either case, because $w_{\sigma(i),\sigma(j)} = w_{i,j}$ for such $\sigma$, so Corollary 1 applies exactly to the class-sum variable. The orbit partition of the subgroup generated by two subgroups is the finest common coarsening of their orbit partitions, whereas the orbit partition of an intersection of subgroups can be strictly finer than the coarsest common refinement of theirs (in $D_3$ acting on the six states of Fig. 1, $\langle r \rangle \cap \langle s \rangle = \{e\}$ has singleton orbits while the common refinement is the orbit partition of $\langle s \rangle$); Fig. 1B shows that the map from subgroups to partitions is not injective. Endnote [39] is reversed if read literally: since $b^*_t = \mathbb{E}\{P_{\phi_G} B_t \mid \vec{V}_t\}$ (Eqs. 24-25), the law of total variance gives $b^*_t$ the smaller variance as a random vector, so the statement is correct only if it refers to the spread of the posterior over $z_t$, which is on average larger under $b^*_t$ because $\vec{V}_t$ is a function of $\vec{Y}_t$.

## Relevance to this research

> [!note] Editorial: relation to the gauge-theoretic multi-agent free energy program
> Two statements in the paper are exact within its setting and bear on the account of meta-agents in [[Meta-agents and hierarchical emergence]]. A Markov kernel that commutes with a group action has an informationally closed quotient on orbits (Corollary 1), and when the observation channel and the prior share the symmetry, Bayesian filtering of the orbit variable given the orbit measurements is exact (Theorem 2, Corollary 3); this is the posterior given the full measurements only when Eq. (29) holds (Corollary 4). In a finite, discrete-state setting this is a sufficient condition for the kind of exact closure that [[Approximate meta-agent theorem]] replaces by declared models with measured defects; that page concerns linear-Gaussian dynamics, to which the paper's theorems do not apply as stated, so the relation is an analogy and not an implication. Corollary 4 with the Ehrenfest counterexample is a caution: a closed macroscopic level does not make the coarse belief the marginal of the fine belief unless Eq. (29) holds. Everything beyond that is interpretive. The paper's macroscopic variable is a group invariant (an orbit label), whereas the program's coarse-graining of transported Gaussian beliefs is described in the vault as a covariant map, so the nearer analogue of $\phi_G$ would be the gauge-invariant content of a configuration; that reading is editorial. The paper has no counterpart of gauge frames, of parallel transport between agents, or of attention weights computed from KL divergences.
>
> The gaps are large. The theorems concern a finite group acting globally on a discrete state space in discrete time, with a Reynolds average normalized by $|G|$. The program has continuous groups (for a noncompact group such as the [[GL(K) gauge group]] no normalized Haar average exists), continuous belief states, and local gauge freedom with one group element per agent. A per-agent action is formally a product group acting on the joint state, which the finite theory would cover only if each factor were finite, but whether the program's belief dynamics is an equivariant Markov kernel under such an action, and whether orbit spaces of continuous actions support the same closure and filtering statements, is not addressed by the paper, and no such result is claimed here. The paper also obtains exactness by restricting the prediction target and contrasts this with variational approximation, which is the program's method.
