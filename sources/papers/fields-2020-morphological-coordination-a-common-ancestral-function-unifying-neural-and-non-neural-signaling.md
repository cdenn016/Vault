## Contribution and scope

Fields, Bischof, and Levin review evidence that neural and non-neural signaling share an older role in coordinating cellular behavior during morphogenesis. Their proposed ancestral function is long-range coordination of cell division, differentiation, and movement needed to build and maintain body-axis organization. This extends accounts of nervous-system evolution centered on whole-animal sensation and movement to the decisions of cells and tissues. The authors compare diffuse biochemical and bioelectric signals with the spatially and temporally selective delivery made possible by neuronal processes. They discuss developmental and regenerative evidence and suggest that neural signaling complements other mechanisms controlling form. The historical claim that this coordination helped enable complex animal bodies is an evolutionary hypothesis supported by comparative arguments, not a directly observed reconstruction of the origin of nervous systems. The review also identifies substantial gaps in experimental work on neural activity during morphogenesis.

## Relevance to this research

This provides biological context for [[Collective active inference]] and [[Meta-agents and hierarchical emergence]]: a communication channel can coordinate component behavior toward a collective morphology without every component representing that morphology in the same way.
