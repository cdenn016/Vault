## Contribution and scope

Fields and Levin interpret regulative development as an interaction between a system and an environment that each constrain the other's behavior. Within their free-energy account, an environment can be described as training or engineering a system toward larger-scale, more predictable forms. The paper combines thermodynamic resource requirements, bidirectional information exchange, and multiscale competency architectures to explain why assembling simpler components into organized structures can be advantageous. Development, regeneration, synthetic organisms, and origin-of-life scenarios become related instances of environment-assisted organization. The environmental description is an inferential interpretation of physical interactions; it need not imply a human-like intention. The strongest proposal is that this setting generically favors increasing morphological complexity and makes living organization thermodynamically favorable. That proposal is tied to the paper's boundary and free-energy assumptions, rather than established by a quantitative comparison covering all possible environments or a new origin-of-life experiment.

## Relevance to this research

This develops an existing formation intuition relevant to [[Meta-agents and hierarchical emergence]]: components acquire organization through their mutual environments, rather than from an isolated internal objective alone. For [[Gauge-Theoretic Multi-Agent VFE Model]], an exact version would need the interacting laws, resource constraints, and formation dynamics specified. Treating the environment as an agent does not automatically provide a normalized common objective or a causal replacement for all retained lower-level context.
