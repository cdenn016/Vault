# Real Effects of Information Frictions: When the States and the Kingdom Became United

> [!info] Citation
> Steinwender, C. (2018). *Real Effects of Information Frictions: When the States and the Kingdom Became United*. American Economic Review, 108(3), 657-696. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20150681)

No local copy: no open-access file passed validation on November 10, 2026. Reading basis: abstract.

## TL;DR

The paper uses the establishment of the transatlantic telegraph in 1866 as a historical experiment to estimate how information frictions distort international trade.

## Problem & setting

The author collects data on cotton prices, trade and information flows from historical newspapers.

## Method

A trade model in which exporters use the latest news about a foreign market to forecast expected prices is fitted to the data.

## Key results

Per the abstract, the mean and the volatility of the transatlantic price difference fell after the telegraph, while average trade flows rose and became more volatile. The estimated efficiency gain is equivalent to 8 percent of export value.

## Relevance to this research

The manuscript cites the study as empirical evidence that a faster communication link between two markets reduced both the mean and the volatility of the price difference between New York and Liverpool. This bears on how communication speed affects coherence between spatially separated agents; see [[Active inference communication and collective boundaries]], [[Communication-constrained inference]] and [[Information cascades and herding]].
