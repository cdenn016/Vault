# Understanding and sharing intentions: The origins of cultural cognition

> [!info] Citation
> Tomasello, M., Carpenter, M., Call, J., Behne, T., Moll, H. (2005). *Understanding and sharing intentions: The origins of cultural cognition*. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 28(5), 675-691. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X05000129)

No local copy: no open-access file passed validation on November 10, 2026. Reading basis: abstract.

## TL;DR

A target article proposing that the difference between human cognition and that of other species is shared intentionality, the ability to take part in collaborative activities with shared goals and intentions.

## Problem & setting

The abstract argues that shared intentionality underlies species-unique cultural cognition, including linguistic symbols, social norms and institutions.

## Method

The authors present evidence that great apes, and some children with autism, understand intentional action but do not take part in joint intentions and attention. They describe human infants' skills as developing over the first 14 months from two intertwining pathways, understanding others as goal-directed agents and a motivation to share experience.

## Relevance to this research

The manuscript cites this paper, with Harnad, for grounding meaning in shared attention: a receiver can learn a sender's code from co-occurrence of messages with its own observations. It names joint attention as a conceptual antecedent only. The manuscript read the abstract.
